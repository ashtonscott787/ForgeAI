Eight people.
Eight very different days.

The same app in all eight. What changes is what you point it at — your mailbox, your spreadsheets, your phone line, your own software. Here is what that actually looks like.

A winery cellar door · Hawke’s Bay · 4 staff

The phone stops being a tax on the afternoon.

Eleven calls a day, nine of them the same: are you open, do you do tastings, can I get a case to Auckland. The line picks up on the second ring, offers a short menu, and answers from the tasting notes and price list already in Knowledge. Anyone who asks for a person gets put through to the mobile in the shed. Anyone calling after five leaves a message that is typed out and waiting in the morning, with the number to ring back.

The website chat answers the same questions in the same words, because it reads the same notes. When someone asks something nobody has written down, the question goes on a list — and ForgeAI goes and finds out the ones that are not about the business.

What is switched on

  • A phone line with a three-item menu and voicemail
  • Website chat, under a name rather than “Chat with us”
  • The wine list and price list as Knowledge
  • A free model on the office Mac — nothing leaves the building

A freight depot · 2 sites · 30 staff

A screen on the wall that only shows what is going wrong.

The dispatch system has an API nobody ever had time to use. ForgeAI is given the handful of things it is allowed to ask it — list today’s consignments, look one up, check a tracking number — and nothing else. Every morning an agent walks the open jobs, checks each one against the courier’s own tracking page, and writes the exceptions to a page that lives on a monitor above the pick line: what has not left, what is late, what failed delivery.

Customers whose parcel is stuck get a short, plain message drafted and waiting for someone to press send. Nobody has to open the dispatch system to find out the day is going badly.

What is switched on

  • Their dispatch software, over its API, read-only
  • The courier’s tracking pages, through a real browser
  • The Orders and shipping agent pack
  • A page it builds and re-publishes, shown on a wall monitor

A plumber · one van · no office

Quotes written between jobs, from the real price list.

The price list is a spreadsheet that has been added to for nine years. It is now a data connection ForgeAI can read but can never change. A quote starts as two sentences dictated from the van — “replace the hot water cylinder at the Napier job, 250 litre, mains pressure” — and comes back as a proper quote with the right line items and the right prices, ready to send.

At the end of the month the overdue invoices get chased, politely at a week and firmly at sixty days, every one of them left as a draft for a human to read before it goes.

What is switched on

  • The price list spreadsheet folder, read-only
  • The mailbox, with every reply left as a draft
  • Speaking instead of typing, from the phone
  • The Bookkeeping helper agent pack

A secondary school art room · two aging PCs

Thirty students, two small graphics cards, no cloud account.

Nothing a student types leaves the school. A small model runs on the art-room machines; the teacher’s Mac does the heavier work and the PCs join it over the school network. Lesson plans come with the timing worked out and something for the ones who finish early. Feedback on a folio is drafted — one thing that worked, one change that would help — and the teacher edits it before any of it reaches a student.

Each class is its own workspace with its own icon, its own files and its own memory, so Year 11 and Year 13 never see each other’s work.

What is switched on

  • A small free model, running locally on each machine
  • The Mac as the main computer, the PCs joined to it
  • A workspace per class, kept apart
  • The Classroom agent pack

An accountant · 90 small clients

The February pile, read before anyone opens it.

Clients email receipts as photographs at midnight. Each one is read, the supplier and the total and the GST pulled out, and anything where the numbers do not add up is flagged rather than quietly recorded. The ledger is connected read-only, so ForgeAI can answer “what did this client spend on fuel last quarter” without anyone being able to change a figure through it.

Each client is a separate workspace. Nothing one client’s AI knows can reach another’s.

What is switched on

  • The ledger database, read-only and enforced by the driver
  • A mailbox per client, watched
  • A workspace per client, with its own data and limits
  • Who may see which connection, set per person

A physio clinic · 3 practitioners

A link patients can just talk to.

Instead of a chat bubble bolted to the website, the clinic sends people a link. It opens a full page in their own name — what the clinic charges, what ACC covers, where to park, how to move an appointment. It answers in a couple of short messages at a human pace, not as a wall of bullet points.

It will not guess. If someone asks whether a particular injury needs a referral, it says that needs a person and takes their number.

What is switched on

  • A shareable chat page, under the clinic’s own name
  • Tap-to-start questions for the usual five
  • Hand off to a person, with the conversation attached
  • Every chat kept in its own inbox, away from staff chats

A small manufacturer · 18 staff · one floor

What is due today, on the wall, without anyone updating it.

The ERP is the kind that was written for one company and never updated. It does have an API, and it is now one of ForgeAI’s connections: six named things it is allowed to ask, and two it is allowed to change — each of which stops and asks a person first, every time. Nothing else on that system is reachable, because it is not on the list.

A page on the wall shows today’s jobs, what is short, and what is waiting on a part. Stock that will run out inside three weeks gets flagged with the actual numbers — fourteen left, five a week — rather than a vague warning.

What is switched on

  • Their ERP, over its API, as a named list of allowed actions
  • Anything that changes data stops and asks first
  • A stock watch that reports numbers, not adjectives
  • A built page on a wall monitor, refreshed on a schedule

A property manager · 140 doors

Maintenance requests that turn into jobs by themselves.

Tenants email. Some of it is urgent, most of it is not, and all of it arrives as a paragraph with no address in it. Each one is read, matched to a property, sorted into urgent and not, and turned into a job with the tradesperson who normally does that street. The reply to the tenant is drafted in the manager’s own voice, saying what will happen and roughly when.

Anything about money, a dispute or a notice to leave is never answered automatically — it is flagged and left alone.

What is switched on

  • The shared maintenance mailbox, watched
  • The property list as a read-only spreadsheet folder
  • The Front desk agent pack, with its own rules added
  • Nothing sent without a person pressing send

The common thread

None of them had an IT department.

Every one of these is a person who already does their own admin, pointing the same app at their own mess. The setup is an afternoon, not a project. And because it runs on their own machines, the mailbox, the spreadsheet and the customer list never leave the building.
